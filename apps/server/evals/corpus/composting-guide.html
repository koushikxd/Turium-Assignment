Hot composting at home, step by step

Cold composting works, eventually. A heap left to itself will turn into compost in a year or two. Hot composting gets you there in six to eight weeks, and it kills most weed seeds on the way. The price is a bit of attention: the right mix, the right size and turning it regularly.

Browns and greens

Everything you add is either a brown or a green. Browns are dry and rich in carbon: autumn leaves, straw, shredded cardboard, wood chips. Greens are moist and rich in nitrogen: grass clippings, vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, fresh manure from grazing animals. A hot heap wants roughly three parts brown to one part green by volume. Too many greens and it turns slimy and smells of ammonia; too many browns and it simply sits there, cold and dry.

Chop things small. A whole cabbage stalk will still be recognisable in two months, while the same stalk cut into thumb-sized pieces disappears in weeks, because the microbes work on surfaces and small pieces have more of them.

Size matters

The heap has to be big enough to hold its own heat. Aim for at least one cubic metre, about a metre on every side. Smaller heaps lose heat through their surface faster than the microbes can make it, and never get properly hot. Building it all at once is better than adding a bucket a day, so save up browns in bags through autumn and greens in a lidded bin for a week or two.

Water and air

The inside of the heap should feel like a wrung-out sponge: damp all the way through, but no water drips out when you squeeze a handful. Water each layer as you build. The microbes that make the heat need oxygen too, which is why you turn the heap.

Temperature and turning

Within two or three days the centre should reach 55 to 65 degrees Celsius. A long compost thermometer is cheap and takes the guessing out of it. Once the temperature starts to fall, usually after four or five days, turn the heap: move the outside to the middle and the middle to the outside, and add water if it looks dry. It will heat up again. Repeat each time it cools, and after four or five turns it will stop reheating. That is the sign that the hot phase is over.

Do not let it go above 70 degrees for long. At that point even the useful microbes start to die off. If it gets that hot, turn it straight away and mix in more browns.

What to leave out

Meat, fish, dairy and cooked food attract rats, even in a hot heap. Leave out dog and cat waste, because of the parasites they can carry. Diseased plants and the roots of perennial weeds such as bindweed are fine only if your heap reliably stays above 60 degrees for several days, which most home heaps do not; bin them instead.

Curing

When the heap no longer reheats, let it sit for another month to cure before using it. Fresh compost that is still breaking down can rob seedlings of nitrogen. Finished compost is dark, crumbly and smells like a forest floor.
